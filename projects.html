F.O.C.A.: Original Spacecraft Mission Design

Jan to May 2026 • Spacecraft Systems Designer • spacecraft structures, mechanism design, design for manufacture, trade studies, systems engineering

Original LEO interceptor concept for net-and-tether capture of uncooperative tumbling objects, carried through SRR, SDR, and PDR review gates. This entry covers the structure, capture mechanism, pressure system, thermal design, and manufacturing plan.

Project details

Overview

F.O.C.A. (Foreign Object Capture & Analysis) is a 300 kg class, monopropellant LEO spacecraft designed to capture and stabilize an uncooperative object. The target is a tumbling, non-responsive body of 1 to 3 m characteristic size and up to 200 kg, with no grapple fixtures, no docking interface, and no attitude cooperation. Capture is by deployable net and tether, followed by closed-loop reel-in to a 2 to 3 m hold length and an optical characterization phase.

I worked the project as an individual contributor and owned the vehicle architecture, primary structure and packaging, capture mechanism concept, mass budget, trade studies, and the SRR, SDR, and PDR documentation. This entry covers the structural, mechanical, and manufacturing side of that work. Avionics, navigation, and ground segment design are documented in the PDR but are not detailed here.

The architecture is conservative on the bus and aggressive in one place. Propulsion, power, attitude control, communications, and thermal hardware are all flight-proven classes (TRL 9). Development risk is concentrated in the net and tether payload, because that element determines mission success and needs the most verification effort. The net concept is baselined on the RemoveDEBRIS in-flight demonstration.

Vehicle architecture

The bus is a monolithic aluminum honeycomb sandwich box. All subsystems are hosted inside or on this structure, with the net launcher on the negative Y face, aligned with the close approach corridor, and an identical backup net stowed on the opposite face.

Packaging followed three constraints. The net deployment axis has to stay clear of the arrays and antennas. The reel and net mechanism mount to a stiffened internal shear panel so tether loads go into primary structure. Thruster cant angles are set so the thrust centroid passes through the center of mass within the accuracy of mass property knowledge, which limits parasitic torque during translation.

Primary structure

The primary structure is an aluminum honeycomb sandwich box with internal shear panels that carry the propulsion module, avionics stack, wheel assembly, and net deployment system. Launch quasi-static and random vibration loads govern sizing, not the on-orbit environment.

Local stiffening is added at the launch vehicle separation interface, the propellant tank mount, and the net mechanism mount. These are the concentrated load paths, and the stiffening also keeps natural frequencies above launch vehicle requirements. The design achieves a factor of safety of 1.5 at all interfaces against a 1.4 requirement. Carbon fiber face sheets are carried as a mass-reduction option pending a cost trade.

Net and tether capture mechanism

The capture payload is the only custom-development element on the vehicle. An 8 m net of approximately 10 kg is launched at about 10 m/s by a spring stack or cold gas charge. Four 0.3 to 0.5 kg corner masses carry the net outward and keep it tensioned in free flight. After envelopment, a Dyneema or Kevlar tether rated to 1000 N transfers load to a brushless-motor reel inside the bus.

The reel retracts at approximately 0.02 m/s and holds 20 to 50 N of regulated tension using load cell feedback at the tether attachment. A designed weak link allows autonomous tether release if tension passes a hard limit. The package stows in roughly 200 x 200 x 300 mm at 12 to 15 kg including the launcher, and it is allocated 30 kg with a 250 kg dynamic capture capacity against the 200 kg target requirement (+25%).

Design problems the mechanism has to solve
  • Deployment dynamics. The net must go from packed to fully expanded with low dispersion, governed by impulse and angular momentum conservation. At 600 km, drag over a sub-second deployment is negligible. The dominant uncertainty is rotational dispersion of the corner masses about the net axis: if they do not reach the design plane together, the net opens asymmetrically and effective capture area drops.
  • Contact dynamics. Too high a relative velocity at contact overloads individual filaments; too low, and the target can pass through before closure. Filament choice trades tensile strength against mass and packing volume. UHMWPE (Dyneema) exceeds 2,400 MPa tensile strength at a density well below steel, and mesh geometry balances porosity against the load capacity of individual strands.
  • Post-capture dynamics. The enveloped target, tether, and chaser form a flexible multibody system. The control problems are slack-taut cycling, tether vibration, and coupling between target tumble and chaser attitude, which is sensitive to a target inertia tensor the chaser cannot observe.
Design decisions that reduce the risk
  • Oversized capture envelope. An 8 m net against a 1 to 3 m target tolerates several meters of pose error at release.
  • Monte Carlo tuned corner masses. Mass distribution is tuned so that even under worst-case pre-release rotation dispersion, the net opens to at least 70% of nominal area before reaching the target.
  • Tether strength margin of approximately 10 to 1 against maximum expected dynamic contact load, so filament rupture is not the limiting failure mode.
  • Closed-loop reel control. A load cell at the tether attachment throttles reel speed, slowing when tension exceeds 50 N and speeding up when it falls below 20 N. This prevents the slack-taut cycling that an open-loop scheme would allow.
Mechanical redundancy

A backup net is stowed on the opposite face. If the first net fails to envelop, the vehicle backs off, jettisons the deployed net through a tether release, and arms the second net. Separately, if tension passes a hard bound that indicates fragmentation or imminent filament rupture, the weak link severs, the vehicle enters safe mode, and it performs a collision avoidance maneuver.

Propulsion and pressure system

The baseline is a monopropellant hydrazine system. Two thruster classes share the feed system: 20 N thrusters for phasing and rendezvous maneuvers, and 1 N thrusters for fine control during proximity operations and tether stabilization. Valves are cross-strapped, which gives one level of fault tolerance: a single valve failure does not prevent completion of rendezvous, though it can erode delta-V margin. Catalyst beds are electrically preheated before firing, because cold starts cut specific impulse and produce thrust transients that are unacceptable during fine proximity maneuvering.

Propellant is held in a titanium-lined, carbon-overwrapped pressure vessel of approximately 50 L, operated at 20 to 30 bar with a burst margin near 1.5 times maximum expected operating pressure and a diaphragm or bladder for positive expulsion. Propellant mass is sized with the Tsiolkovsky equation:

Δv = Isp · g0 · ln(m0 / mf)   ⇒   m0 / mf = exp[ Δv / (Isp · g0) ]

With a 350 m/s requirement and an Isp of 220 s, the mass ratio is 1.176, which needs about 43 kg of propellant at 290 kg wet. The design carries 44 kg for dispersion and the cold-start Isp penalty, which gives approximately 369 m/s of capability at 236 kg dry.

Thermal design

Thermal control is predominantly passive: MLI on most external surfaces, second-surface mirror radiators on selected panels, and thermal interface materials at high-dissipation mounts. Active control is limited to heaters with redundant controllers on the propellant tank, the hydrazine catalyst beds, and the LIDAR mount.

Two cases drive the design. The hot case combines high solar flux, full power during capture, and minimum deep-space view, with radiators rejecting approximately 200 W continuous plus reel motor peak load. The cold case is post-deployment safe mode, where loads fall to survival minimum and survival heaters hold the tank and catalyst beds above minimum temperature. Heater power carries at least 50% reserve against the worst cold case, and component temperature margin is greater than 5 degrees C in all cases.

Design trade studies

Three trades were carried through PDR: capture mechanism, propulsion technology, and launch vehicle. Each used a weighted attribute matrix with a stated selection rationale. The working rule was that the ranking informs the decision but a hard requirement governs it: where the highest raw score belonged to an option that cannot meet a requirement, the narrative says so and the option is rejected on that basis.

Capture flexibility carried the heaviest weight, since the mission premise is a target of unknown geometry and tumble state. The net captures by entanglement over a large envelope, which tolerates pose error and yields the lowest fragmentation risk. The arm needs tight alignment against a tumbling body and costs roughly four times the power and 15 kg more mass. The harpoon is simpler to deploy but penetrates the target and carries the highest risk of structural damage and new debris.

Bipropellant has the best specific impulse but adds dual propellant storage, ignition and thermal complexity, and mass. Cold gas is light and simple, and it scores well on mass, cost, thermal, and power attributes, but its specific impulse cannot close a roughly 350 m/s delta-V budget at a reasonable propellant mass. That requirement overrides its raw score. Monopropellant hydrazine is the compromise: adequate efficiency, flight heritage, and a simple, robust architecture suited to responsive proximity operations.

Launch vehicle

Electron, Firefly Alpha, and Falcon 9 Transporter were scored on cost, mass capacity margin, insertion precision, schedule, heritage, and integration complexity. Alpha and Transporter tied at 0.74 against 0.66 for Electron, so the decision rested on engineering margin. Electron (approx. 300 kg to LEO) leaves inadequate growth margin against the upper end of the wet mass range. Transporter is the cost leader, but the drop-off orbit is set by the primary customer and circularizing at 600 km would consume delta-V from a margin that is already 5.7%.

Recommendation: Firefly Alpha as baseline (approx. 1030 kg to LEO), for mass margin and dedicated control of the insertion orbit. Falcon 9 Transporter is the cost-driven alternative, conditional on circularization delta-V fitting inside the propulsion margin.

What I learned

  • Numbers have to close across documents. Mass and delta-V figures appear in the budget, the sizing calculation, and the trade studies. Keeping them consistent, and tracking margins against a policy that tightens at each review, is a configuration control task in itself.
  • A trade score informs the decision but does not make it. The propulsion trade was decided by a hard delta-V requirement over the raw ranking, and the launch vehicle trade ended in a tie that only mass growth and insertion control could resolve.
  • Concentrate development risk deliberately. Using flight-proven hardware everywhere except the capture payload keeps verification budget on the element that decides mission success.
  • Tether and net dynamics are a coupling problem. Deployment symmetry, contact load, and post-capture control interact, and the target inertia tensor cannot be observed from the chaser. That drove the oversized net, the tension-feedback reel, and the tumble-rate limit.
  • Design for recovery, not only the nominal case. A backup net and a weak-link tether release mean no single mechanical failure ends the mission or the vehicle.

Robotic Bat Research

Aug 2025 – May 2026 • Design & Fabrication Lead, Master's Project • biomimetic robotics, ornithopter, design for additive manufacturing, tolerance analysis, gear train design, materials selection

A bat-inspired ornithopter for remote water sampling, designed and built from concept through fabrication and test, including its structure, circuitry, motors, and wing membrane. Gear train, front plate, and wing attachment redesign supported the first powered flapping demonstration on the platform.

Project details

Overview

The bat robot is an ornithopter built around the drinking maneuver of a bat. In that maneuver the animal flies low over water and shapes its wings into a concave surface that compresses the air column beneath it against the water, which produces the lift that holds it in position for the brief tongue extension. The platform is intended for remote water sample collection in locations that fixed wing and rotary wing aircraft cannot reach, and as an experimental platform for studying the aerodynamics of the maneuver.

The platform was inherited from several prior teams and had three problems that blocked sustained flight. The gear actuated flapping mechanism failed at high RPM through slippage and tooth breakage. The wing material was still in iteration, and the urethane and silicone polymers tested previously tore or added more distributed load than the actuation could lift. The flight mechanism also had no pitch or yaw authority. The chassis had already been optimized for motor, battery, and gear stack placement and was kept as received.

The goal was to establish reliable, repeatable flapping before adding mechanical complexity. My work covered the interface between the gear train and its printed structure, the wing spar attachments, the wing membrane material screening, and the circuit and motor integration. I also built a wing folding prototype and added airfoil sections to prepare for more bat-like kinematics.

The integrated prototype drove its wings under power and produced a recognizable flapping motion on a test stand. It did not achieve free flight. The copper gear train ran the full test without the tooth shearing seen in earlier iterations, which closed out the gear material question for the platform.

Drivetrain: gear material and geometry

The gear train converts motor output into the flapping stroke. The motor turns continuously at a speed set by the ESC, and the gear train reduces that rotation to a flapping frequency suited to the wing geometry. The spars are driven directly from the gear train at each wing root. For both wings to move consistently, the train has to stay aligned, transmit motion smoothly, and survive the friction generated in operation.

Material progression

The first generation used off-the-shelf plastic gears, which were not strong enough for the friction and loading in the mechanism and failed quickly. The second generation used resin printed gears. Resin allowed changes to gear ratio, tooth depth, and tooth count, so the tooth count was reduced from 50 to 36. The deeper, more engaged teeth were meant to mesh more securely, and the lower tooth count was meant to reduce friction. A deeper pitch angle was also used to keep the gears interlocked.

The resin gears were useful for prototyping gear profiles quickly. They outperformed the plastic gears but still suffered tooth damage under sustained friction, and the longer print times made iteration slow. They worked as a prototyping step and not as the final gear set.

The third generation used copper gears that were already available in the lab from a previous project. They were thicker, smaller, and heavier than the resin gears, which raised three concerns before use: whether they would mesh with the smaller plastic gears still in the system, whether the metal would wear the plastic teeth, and whether the press fit onto the rods, with no set screws, would hold. In testing they meshed well with the remaining plastic gears, withstood more friction than the resin gears, and reduced slipping. Their cost was added mass and thickness, and a change in gear dimensions that altered the gear ratio and forced a front plate redesign.

Front plate and tolerance characterization

Because the metal gears changed spacing and layout, the front plate was redesigned around the actual gear positions instead of forcing the new gears into the old layout. The plate repositions the rods, bearings, spacers, and gear holes, and its job is to hold the gears in alignment. When the plate was not stiff or properly toleranced, the gears bowed away from each other under vibration and load, which caused skipping, slipping, and uneven wing motion.

Six plate iterations were printed beyond the inherited design. The inherited CAD had no clearances applied to its holes, which is a problem at the resolution of a desktop printer, and PLA expansion had to be factored in as well. The structural rods also had to thread through the housings. The prints came from the Rapid Prototyping Lab and were often delayed, so each correction after an error was costly in schedule, and that limited how many iterations were practical.

Wing spar attachments and membrane materials

The wing attachment design started with material selection. PLA and TPU were both printed, and PLA was chosen for its higher resolution at small sizes and faster print times. Each attachment needs a hole through which the polymer can enter and cure, so the bond is both adhesive and mechanical.

The attachments were first designed as one solid piece per side. Several weight-reduction patterns were tried, and some exposed portions of the spar. Because of weight, the design settled on four small individual attachments per side, each with three horizontal holes that let the polymer flow through and maximize adhesion area. The current iteration is too small, and a larger attachment is recommended. As with the gear housings, print delays made each adjustment slow.

Membrane screening

With the attachments resolved, the work turned to the membrane. Polymers were cast in small batches to evaluate their properties and their adhesion to the attachments, and their behavior at different thicknesses was compared. The cast polymers were also too heavy for the fragile actuation system to move.

Wing folding prototype and airfoils

A bat wing is a continuous membrane on an elongated skeletal structure with more than 25 joints. Reproducing that articulation at this scale would put actuator and structure mass where the platform is most sensitive to it, so the wings were simplified to rigid spars with a flexible membrane. To prepare for more bat-like kinematics, I prototyped a wing folding and extension mechanism that models the humerus, radius, carpal, metacarpal, and phalange motion.

The linkage uses revolute and cylindrical joints. As the wing folds into the chassis, the cylindrical joint connecting the humerus and radius slides inward along the humerus, which models the folding of the humerus and the extension of the radius. The joints on the metacarpal and phalange slide along the chassis, so the carpal, metacarpal, and phalange fold in while staying mostly parallel.

The design favored easy iteration for future teams, so it uses parts already on the robot and prints the rest. The bars are carbon fiber rods: the thicker wing rods for the radius and phalange, which see the most rotation and translation, and the thinner chassis support rods for the metacarpals. The sliders are PLA parts fit to the rod diameter and the bearing outer diameter. PLA end caps seat the rods in bearings, and pins join the bearings. The phalange rod attaches to the chassis through a spring and rod damper that allows multi-axial rotation and translation, so rod lengths can change without locking the linkage.

Three constraints shaped the prototype. This copy of the robot was reserved for flight actuation, so the humerus bar had to stay solid for wing attachment and a cylindrical slider was needed to emulate its shortening. The spring and rod damper had to stay so that electronics and chassis components could be repositioned later. The geometry itself also limits travel: the shoulder pieces protrude 1.8 in from the gear actuation system, the electronics carrier restricts motion along the y axis to 5.5 in, and the upper metacarpal slide is limited to 2.5 in between the motor attachment and the electronics holder.

The wings also lay flat on the span of the chassis and wing rods, which gives little lift because the underside area is small. Airfoil sections were attached to the front of the wing to address that. They were modeled from NACA 6409 cross sections, printed in white TPU, and held on the wing rods by friction so their placement can be adjusted. The larger section (1.96 in chord) sits 4.5 in from the shoulder, and the smaller one (1.714 in chord) sits 11 in out, forming an under-wing arc. Camber angle was not optimized because electronics issues delayed the flight test.

Integration and test

The final test ran the robot as an integrated prototype to observe the drivetrain and wings under power. It was not intended as a full flight. The robot carried the updated front plate, the metal lower gears, the existing smaller plastic gears, the wing spars, a temporary membrane, and the transmitter and receiver control setup. A 4S battery powered the motor, and the transmitter, receiver, and ESC controlled motor input in place of the earlier Arduino based method.

The stand let the wings and drivetrain move without the body being held by hand, and it gave visibility of the gears, rods, dampers, and temporary stops, all of which were still exposed and adjustable. The ESC did not register until about 30 percent throttle, so the motor was ramped carefully. Testing was done in short runs, with the system stopped between runs to inspect gears, shafts, wing attachments, and damping.

Several fixes were made on the day. The motor axle was too large for its bearing, and running without the bearing allowed unwanted axle motion that disturbed gear alignment. Correcting it meant taking the front plate apart, which was difficult because many parts were tightly press fit after the earlier tolerance work. The axle was sanded slightly so the bearing would fit, and the plate was reassembled. Shaft sliding and spring damping were adjusted as the system ran, and temporary hard stops were added so the mechanism could keep running.

Results
  • Proof of flapping: the platform drove its wings under power and produced a recognizable flapping motion. Earlier teams could not reach this point because their gear assemblies failed before sustained operation.
  • Copper gears validated: no brittle failure or tooth shearing through the duration of the test, and no damage to the retained plastic gears.
  • Suspension damping: the system holding the robot in place added enough damping that the wings vibrated against it and could not reach their full stroke, so the test shows a constrained version of the intended motion.
  • Motor sizing: at its lowest available setting the motor still drove the gear train beyond a flapping speed the platform could use.

Recommendations for the next iteration

Several decisions were validated by the test and should be kept: copper gears at the main drive position, or a lighter metal, and the existing plastic gears elsewhere. The remaining priorities, in order, are:

  1. Re-characterize printed tolerances under load. The values in this entry gave consistent static fits, but several fits still shifted or loosened in operation. They are a starting point and not a final answer.
  2. Replace the motor with a lower power unit. This gives finer control over flapping frequency and lowers the load on the gear engagement and tolerancing.
  3. Redesign the suspension. It needs to provide structural support without imposing damping that degrades the stroke.
  4. Iterate the wing folding mechanism and add actuation. A shorter humerus and more wing rods would allow actual folding and not only sliding, and would make room for more phalanges and metacarpals.
  5. Flight test the silicone membrane with a strong adhesive, and test the airfoil designs for flexibility and effectiveness. Larger spar attachments are also recommended.

The longer term direction is a four bar linkage in place of the direct gear driven stroke, and active coupling of the tail servo to the membrane to add pitch and yaw control. Both should follow the priorities above, because each of those addresses a foundational issue that would complicate more sophisticated mechanisms.

Engineering contradictions and TRIZ

Four contradictions drove the concept, and each one was tracked through the design iterations.

  • Cost against functionality. Smart glasses can replicate the technique at much higher cost and complexity, so the product has to stay cheap, which limits features such as EEG sensing and feedback.

A substance-field model framed the interactions. Five substances were identified: the retina, the LED modules, the lens and frame material, the electronics and cell in the arm, and ambient light. Four fields act between them: optical (the 40 Hz stimulus), thermal, structural, and electrical. The intended path is LED to optical field to retina. The harmful path is LED to thermal field to wearer, since excess heat or radiance can exceed comfort and safety limits. The analysis also flagged ambient light overpowering the signal, limited arm volume forcing uneven LED placement, and mechanical stress on the frame from embedded electronics.

Finite element analysis: tapping

A distributed vertical load on the top face gives a peak displacement of 1.24 × 10-3 mm (1.24 µm) at the center of the face, falling radially toward the rim, and a peak von Mises stress of 3.7 × 105 N/m² (0.37 MPa), also at the center. Finite element analysis: twist

A tangential load applied around the knob rim gives a peak von Mises stress of 2.94 × 107 N/m² (29.4 MPa), about 80 times the tapping case, concentrated at the rim where the load is applied. The body of the shell stays at low stress. Peak displacement is 0.138 mm at the outer edge, growing with radius from the wheel axis, as expected for torsion about that axis.

Against an ABS yield of about 40 MPa, the rim peak is a ratio of about 1.4, and against 6061 it is about 9. A load applied at an edge tends to overpredict local stress, and the plot shows the peak is confined to the loaded rim. The next step for the ABS shell is a refined mesh at the load application, a friction-limited torque from the knob to the hand as the load, and a rib or thicker rim if the peak persists.

ABS housing, injection molded

The shell is designed to the injection molding rules: a uniform 2 mm wall, inside the 2 to 4 mm range that the Ashby process charts mark as favorable, 1 to 2 degrees of draft on vertical surfaces, fillets at corners for flow, and no undercuts that would need side actions. A finish of N7 to N8 (Ra 1.6 to 3.2 µm) comes straight from the mold, with texture and color built into the tool, so no secondary operation is needed. At a planned volume of 1,000,000 units, the SolidWorks costing tool gives a mold cost of $56,058, a total of $0.293 per part including $0.056 of tooling amortization, and about $40,000 for ramp-up and validation.

Aluminum housing, CNC machined

The shell is cut from 2 inch 6061 round bar. At 46.66 cm³ the finished housing has a mass of about 126 g. The design carries threaded inserts for fasteners that survive repeated assembly, and alignment features for the internals. Machining holds a tolerance of ±0.05 mm and an N6 finish (Ra 0.8 µm) before anodizing. The plan is 200,000 units, including a 5 percent defect margin. The costing tool prices the stock at $2.528 per part, since it is billed on the billet and not on the finished mass, with $10,000 for setup and fixtures and $20,000 for programming.

Material selection

The two materials were chosen on Ashby charts for strength against cost and for modulus against density. ABS is the lowest cost polymer on the chart, at about $2.5 per kg and about 40 MPa, and it is light, at 1.05 g/cm³. 6061 sits near the middle of the metals in strength, about 270 MPa at about $4.5 per kg, and it has a specific stiffness (E/ρ) about 12 times that of ABS, which is the basis for the stiffer, more precise feel of the premium variant.

Price positions came from a choice-based conjoint survey. Additional control and price were the two most influential attributes. $50 was the most preferred price point (utility +51.5), preference dropped sharply at $80 and above, and trackpad-integrated formats scored well above scroll-only ones (utility +72.59). That supports a $50 mass-market tier and a clearly differentiated premium tier near $130 to $135.

Market and demand

Any computer user is a possible customer, so the target was narrowed to people who scroll through large files: analysts, developers, accountants, creative professionals, gamers and streamers, and music producers. The scroll wheel market was reported at $1.2 billion in 2023 (DataIntelo).   • Warranty. Full replacement or refund in the first 6 months with no conditions, replacement on verified failure from 6 to 24 months, and discounted upgrades or repair afterward. A later firmware revision could log usage hours over Bluetooth to help verify claims.
  • Safety and environment. Both variants contain a lithium cell and electronics and need proper disposal. Housings use mechanical fasteners and no adhesives, the ABS shell is one polymer with no overmolding, and the aluminum shell is a single alloy that goes through standard scrap channels. Recycled 6061 stock is the planned source for the aluminum variant.
  • Misuse and liability. A wireless device is exposed to eavesdropping and input injection if it is not secured, and open firmware can be reprogrammed for other functions. Ergonomic effects of prolonged use are an open consideration, although the product intent is to reduce fatigue.
  • Intellectual property. The landscape includes rotary controls and touch surfaces from Apple, Microsoft, Logitech, and 3Dconnexion, and each feature has to be designed around related claims. The plan pursues a utility patent for the function and a design patent for the form, holds the firmware that maps rotary input to cursor input as a trade secret, and seeks trademarks for the name, logo, and overall shape. The team filed a patent application.

What I learned

  • Weight the matrix before sizing parts. Encoder and motor accounted for about two thirds of technical importance, which showed where design effort belonged.
  • Average stress hides the load path. The press case has a margin of about 1,900 times, while the twist case concentrates stress at the rim to within a factor of 1.4 of ABS yield. Local peaks, not averages, decided where the shell needed attention.
  • Sensing fields share space. A magnet and a capacitive surface on the same axis interact, so shielding and layer order had to be part of the stack-up from the start.
  • Reliability is a modeling choice until it is tested. The Weibull parameters set the warranty window, and a 41 percent five-year failure estimate for ABS shows why cycle testing comes before volume tooling.
  • One internal assembly, two shells. Sharing the electronics between the ABS and aluminum variants let each housing be priced and toleranced to its own process.
