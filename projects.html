F.O.C.A.: Original Spacecraft Mission Design

Jan to May 2026 • Spacecraft Systems Designer • spacecraft design, rendezvous and proximity operations, systems engineering, trade studies, risk management

Original LEO interceptor concept for autonomous rendezvous, net-and-tether capture, and stabilization of uncooperative tumbling objects, carried through SRR, SDR, and PDR review gates.

Project details

Overview

F.O.C.A. (Foreign Object Capture & Analysis) is a 300 kg class, monopropellant LEO spacecraft designed to demonstrate autonomous rendezvous, capture, and stabilization of an uncooperative object. The target class is a tumbling, non-responsive body of 1 to 3 m characteristic size and up to 200 kg, with no grapple fixtures, no docking interface, and no attitude cooperation. Capture is by deployable net and tether, followed by closed-loop reel-in to a 2 to 3 m hold length and an optical characterization phase with data returned over S-band.

I worked the project as an individual contributor across the full systems engineering stack: top-level objectives and tiered success criteria, concept of operations, subsystem architecture and sizing, mass, power, delta-V, and cost budgets, three formal trade studies, a focused risk analysis on the net and tether capture system, technology readiness assessment, and a CDR plan covering long-lead procurement and make-or-buy decisions. The design was matured through System Requirements Review (SRR), System Definition Review (SDR), and Preliminary Design Review (PDR), and I authored the documentation for each gate.

The architecture is deliberately conservative on the bus and aggressive in one place. Propulsion, power, ADCS, TT&C, and thermal are built from flight-proven hardware classes (TRL 9). Development risk is concentrated in the net and tether payload and the autonomous proximity operations software, because those are the elements that determine mission success and the ones that most need verification budget. The net capture concept is baselined on the RemoveDEBRIS in-flight demonstration.

Mission objectives and success criteria

Four objectives are ranked in decreasing priority: (1) autonomous rendezvous of a 300 kg class vehicle with an uncooperative target at controlled relative velocity, (2) net envelopment of a 1 to 3 m, 200 kg class target without fragmentation, (3) post-capture stabilization that converts a tumbling object into a stably tethered one with bounded relative motion, and (4) on-orbit characterization with downlink to ground. A secondary objective is flight data on net deployment dynamics, tether reel-in control, and reaction force compensation. The mission is framed against IADC space debris mitigation guidelines.

Success is tiered so that partial achievement is still measurable. Each tier maps to storyboard events that are verifiable by telemetry, imagery, or ground tracking.

Concept of operations

The operations concept divides autonomy cleanly. FOCA is fully autonomous within a mission phase and ground-gated between phases. Three authorization gates sit ahead of irreversible or high-consequence events: cruise to proximity operations (G1), the 100 m hold point to the 20 m close approach (G2), and net armed to net deployed (G3). The spacecraft cannot cross a gate on its own. Gate timing is a design driver: the interval between the 100 m characterization and the G2 authorization is held to no more than one orbital period, which sizes the ground pass cadence and same-pass command turnaround.

The ground segment is three elements: a commercial S-band network (KSAT or Atlas class) with 2 to 4 passes per day in cruise and surge capacity to 5 to 8 passes per day during capture; a mission operations center hosting flight dynamics, telemetry processing, the command sequencer, and the duty officer station that issues gate authorizations; and an offline analysis function for pose estimation reconstruction and trend analysis. The link is sized for a worst-case slant range of approximately 2,500 km at 5 degrees elevation, with 4 kbps command and housekeeping on omnidirectional patch antennas in safe mode and up to 1 Mbps science downlink on the steerable low-gain antenna.

Vehicle architecture

The bus is a monolithic aluminum honeycomb sandwich box. All subsystems are hosted inside or on this structure, with the net launcher on the negative Y face, aligned with the close approach corridor, and a backup net stowed on the opposite face.

Subsystem design

Propulsion

The baseline is a monopropellant hydrazine system. Hydrazine decomposes over an iridium-coated alumina catalyst bed, so no ignition system is needed and the architecture stays simple. Two thruster classes share the feed system: 20 N thrusters for phasing and rendezvous maneuvers, and 1 N thrusters for fine control during proximity operations and tether stabilization. Propellant is held in a titanium-lined, carbon-overwrapped pressure vessel of approximately 50 L, operated at 20 to 30 bar with a burst margin near 1.5 times maximum expected operating pressure and a diaphragm or bladder for positive expulsion. Valves are cross-strapped, which gives one level of fault tolerance: a single valve failure does not prevent completion of rendezvous, though it can erode delta-V margin.

Electric propulsion was rejected on thrust level. Close approach corrections and compensation for net ejection reaction and captured-target drag need responsive, high-thrust authority. Several details follow from that choice. Catalyst beds are electrically preheated before firing, because cold starts cut specific impulse and produce thrust transients that are unacceptable during fine proximity maneuvering. Thruster cant angles are set so the thrust centroid passes through the center of mass within the accuracy of mass property knowledge, which limits parasitic torque during translation.

The same relation is used in reverse for operations: remaining delta-V is computed from propellant gauging and compared against the budget reserved for downstream events before each maneuver is committed. This is built into the go and no-go logic at each authorization gate so that no maneuver can consume the margin reserved for capture, reel-in compensation, and end-of-mission disposal.

Power

Two deployable rigid wings with triple-junction GaAs cells (end-of-life efficiency near 28%) supply approximately 250 W at beginning of life against a design average load of approximately 200 W, with the 600 km worst-case eclipse fraction as the sizing case. A lithium-ion battery of about 500 Wh buffers eclipse and peak events (net deployment, reel-in, and continuous attitude control thruster operation) and is sized for roughly 30% depth of discharge in nominal operation, which leaves room for off-nominal events and end-of-life capacity fade. A centralized PMAD unit carries redundant MPPT converters on the array side and a switched, fused load network on the load side, regulated at 28 V. Array release uses a hold-and-release mechanism with redundant non-explosive actuators. The subsystem-level power table sums to a 170 W average and a peak case in proximity operations when LIDAR, reel motor, and thrusters operate concurrently.

Guidance, navigation, and control

Absolute navigation uses GPS augmented by ground tracking. Relative navigation is the most demanding function: a flash imaging LIDAR covers roughly 5 km down to the close approach corridor, and a stereo camera pair supports proximity operations and pose estimation of the target. Pose estimation fuses synchronized LIDAR point clouds and camera frames and runs on the flight computer at 1 Hz or faster (2 Hz demonstrated against a 1 Hz requirement). Required relative position knowledge is sub-meter at close approach, with pose quality sufficient to time net release within milliseconds of the planned window. The guidance law commands a sequence of small impulses that hold a glide slope to the target while keeping relative velocity within budget.

Attitude determination uses a star tracker, a Sun sensor for safe-mode acquisition, and a redundant IMU. Attitude control is a four-wheel reaction wheel pyramid that tolerates a single wheel failure, with magnetorquers for desaturation and thrusters for fast slews and the high-disturbance capture environment. Each wheel stores about 0.5 N·m·s with 0.05 N·m peak torque. Wheel saturation during capture is tracked as a specific risk (T5), since that is the point at which control authority matters most.

Command and data handling

The flight computer is a radiation-tolerant LEON or RAD750 class processor running a real-time operating system, with a redundant pair of mass memory modules sized for several days of high-rate data. A dedicated FPGA hosts the time-critical LIDAR and pose estimation pipeline so burst data rates do not stall the main processor during proximity operations. Flight software is layered (hardware abstraction, flight software framework, mission application) with phase managers, fault detection, isolation and recovery, and a safe mode controller. Safe mode triggers on loss of attitude knowledge beyond threshold, propellant pressure excursion, processor watchdog timeout, or a commanding gap beyond a configurable limit. Exit from safe mode requires a ground command.

Thermal

Thermal control is predominantly passive: MLI on most external surfaces, second-surface mirror radiators on selected panels, and thermal interface materials at high-dissipation mounts. Active control is limited to heaters with redundant controllers on the propellant tank, the hydrazine catalyst beds, and the LIDAR mount. Two cases drive the design. The hot case combines high solar flux, full power during capture, and minimum deep-space view, with radiators rejecting approximately 200 W continuous plus reel motor peak load. The cold case is post-deployment safe mode, where loads fall to survival minimum and survival heaters hold the tank and catalyst beds above minimum temperature. Heater power carries at least 50% reserve against the worst cold case, and component temperature margin is greater than 5 degrees C in all cases.

Structure

The primary structure is an aluminum honeycomb sandwich box, 1.0 x 1.0 x 1.2 m, with internal shear panels carrying the propulsion module, avionics stack, wheel assembly, and net deployment system. Launch quasi-static and random vibration loads govern sizing, not the on-orbit environment. Local stiffening is added at the launch vehicle separation interface, the propellant tank mount, and the net mechanism mount to handle concentrated loads and keep natural frequencies above launch vehicle requirements. The design achieves a factor of safety of 1.5 at all interfaces against a 1.4 requirement. Carbon fiber face sheets are carried as a mass-reduction option pending a cost trade. System-level mass margin follows a declining policy of 30% at SDR, 25% at PDR, 20% at CDR, and 10% at acceptance.

Telemetry, tracking, and command

A coherent S-band transponder with hot spare redundancy handles command, telemetry, and ranging. Two patch antennas on opposite faces give omnidirectional coverage in safe mode, and a steerable low-gain antenna bore-sighted on the anti-target face carries science downlink. Uplink is 4 kbps; downlink is 4 kbps housekeeping and up to 1 Mbps science, with rate selected autonomously from link margin reported at pass acquisition (1.2 Mbps capability, +20% margin). Commercial small-satellite transponders (L3Harris, Syrlinks) cover the need. FCC licensing carries a 6 to 12 month tail and is treated as a long-lead programmatic item to be started at kickoff.

Payload: net and tether capture system

The payload is the only custom-development element on the vehicle. An 8 m net of approximately 10 kg is launched at about 10 m/s by a spring stack or cold gas charge, with four 0.3 to 0.5 kg corner masses that carry the net outward and keep it tensioned in free flight. After envelopment, a Dyneema or Kevlar tether rated to 1000 N transfers load to a brushless-motor reel inside the bus. The reel retracts at approximately 0.02 m/s and holds 20 to 50 N of regulated tension using load cell feedback at the tether attachment. A designed weak link allows autonomous tether release if tension passes a hard limit. The package stows in roughly 200 x 200 x 300 mm at 12 to 15 kg including the launcher, mounted on the negative Y face with the deployment axis along the close approach corridor, and draws about 20 W average and 60 W peak. The payload is allocated 30 kg and is sized for 250 kg dynamic capture capacity against the 200 kg target requirement (+25%).

Trade studies

Three trades were carried through PDR: propulsion technology, capture mechanism, and launch vehicle. Each used a weighted attribute matrix with a stated selection rationale. The working rule was that the quantitative ranking informs the decision but a hard requirement governs it: where the highest raw score belonged to an option that cannot meet a requirement, the narrative says so and the option is rejected on that basis.

Alpha and Falcon 9 Transporter tie on score, so the decision rests on the engineering narrative. Electron (approx. 300 kg to LEO, approx. $7.5M dedicated) leaves inadequate growth margin against the upper end of the wet mass range, since mass grows between PDR and CDR; it stays viable only if wet mass settles below about 280 kg at CDR. Recommendation: Firefly Alpha as baseline, for mass margin and dedicated mission control. Falcon 9 Transporter is the cost-driven alternative, conditional on evaluating circularization delta-V against propulsion margin. Final selection is deferred to PDR closure, based on mass tracking and the available rideshare drop-off altitudes.

Focus technology risk: net and tether capture

The net and tether system is the lowest-maturity element and the one that determines mission success, so it received a dedicated analysis. The risk decomposes into three coupled physics problems.

  • Deployment dynamics. The net must go from packed to fully expanded in a deterministic, low-dispersion manner, governed by impulse and angular momentum conservation. At 600 km the aerodynamic drag on the net over a sub-second deployment is negligible. The dominant uncertainty is rotational dispersion of the corner masses about the net axis: if they do not reach the design plane together, the net opens asymmetrically and effective capture area drops.
  • Contact dynamics. Too high a relative velocity at contact overloads individual filaments and tears the net; too low, and the target can pass through before closure. Filament choice trades tensile strength against mass and packing volume. UHMWPE (Dyneema) exceeds 2,400 MPa tensile strength at a density well below steel. Mesh geometry balances porosity, which affects deployment, against the load capacity of individual strands in entanglement.
  • Post-capture dynamics. The enveloped target, tether, and chaser form a flexible multibody system. The control problems are slack-taut cycling, tether vibration, and coupling between target tumble and chaser attitude. Coupling is sensitive to the target inertia tensor, which the chaser cannot observe directly.
Design decisions that reduce the risk
  • Oversized capture envelope. An 8 m net against a 1 to 3 m target tolerates several meters of pose error at release.
  • Monte Carlo tuned corner masses. Mass distribution is tuned so that even under worst-case pre-release rotation dispersion, the net opens to at least 70% of nominal area before reaching the target.
  • Tether strength margin of approximately 10 to 1 against maximum expected dynamic contact load, so filament rupture is not the limiting failure mode.
  • Closed-loop reel control. A load cell at the tether attachment throttles reel speed in real time, slowing when tension exceeds 50 N and speeding up when it falls below 20 N. This prevents the slack-taut cycling that an open-loop scheme would allow as the tumbling target excites tether dynamics. The control law is modeled as a constrained flexible-body problem and simulated against coupled rotational and translational motion of both bodies.
In-mission mitigations

Residual risk is handled operationally with six baselined mitigations, which together turn a single-point failure into a managed risk with multiple recovery paths:

  1. Pre-deployment characterization at the 100 m hold. Optical and LIDAR data give tumble rate, geometry, and surface properties, which ground reviews before authorizing entry. If tumble rate exceeds 5 deg/s per axis, capture is deferred and the vehicle holds.
  2. Adaptive reel control using tether tension feedback, as above.
  3. Abort and retry. If the first net fails to envelop, the vehicle backs off to the 100 m hold, jettisons the deployed net through a tether release, and arms an identical backup net stored on the opposite face.
  4. Autonomous tether release. If tension passes a hard bound that indicates fragmentation or imminent filament rupture, a designed weak point is severed. The vehicle then enters safe mode, performs a collision avoidance maneuver, and awaits ground commanding.
  5. Ground authorization gates (G1 to G3) ahead of each high-consequence transition.
  6. Redundant sensing. Pose estimation runs on LIDAR and stereo imagery simultaneously; a discrepancy above tolerance commands a hold instead of a deployment.

Technology readiness

TRL was assessed per technology against a CDR target. Technologies at TRL 5 and above are generally acceptable for new mission designs; those below require additional risk reduction before commitment. The bus technologies are TRL 9. The risk sits in the payload and in the integrated capture sequence, where the principal maturation work is bringing each element to TRL 6 by CDR.

CDR plan: long-lead items and make-or-buy

Long-lead items were identified and given purchase order release dates relative to PDR so that procurement does not become the schedule critical path. LIDAR and COPV orders release at PDR plus 3 months, reaction wheels at PDR plus 6 months, and propulsion hardware at PDR plus 9 months. The net and reel mechanism begins prototype development immediately after PDR closure, with a hardware-in-the-loop deployment test article delivered before CDR. Risk reduction to CDR focuses on vacuum deployment kinematics and contact load envelope tests, closed-loop simulation of the reel control law against realistic target dynamics, and integrated rehearsal of the abort and retry sequence on the flight processor with hardware in the loop.

What I learned

  • Numbers have to close across documents. Mass, power, and delta-V figures appear in the budget, the sizing calculation, the trade studies, and the requirements table. Keeping them consistent, and tracking margins against a policy that tightens at each review, is a configuration control task in itself.
  • A trade score informs the decision but does not make it. The propulsion trade was decided by a hard delta-V requirement over the raw ranking, and the launch vehicle trade ended in a tie that only the mass-growth and insertion-control arguments could resolve. I now state the rejection logic explicitly in the matrix narrative.
  • Concentrate development risk deliberately. Using TRL 9 hardware everywhere except the payload and autonomy software keeps verification budget on the elements that decide mission success.
  • Design for recovery, not only for the nominal case. Abort and retry with a backup net, a weak-link tether release, dual-sensor cross-checks, and ground gates mean no single failure ends the mission or the vehicle. Each one bounds a specific failure chain and is tied to a specific risk in the register.
